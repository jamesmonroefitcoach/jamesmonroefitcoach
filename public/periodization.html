The five dials

Every set you do is described by five numbers: how heavy, how many reps, how many sets, how long you rest, and how hard you push. Change the goal and you change the numbers. Nothing below is a rule for its own sake, it is the range the research keeps landing on.

Frequency. Every major muscle group gets trained at least twice a week. Hitting that consistently matters more than which split you pick or how clever the plan looks.1

Reading your own effort

"Reps in reserve" is how many more you honestly could have done when you racked it. It is the dial that keeps a hard week hard and an easy week easy, so learn to call it out loud.

Reps leftWhat it feels likeWhere it belongs
0 leftThe bar stopped moving. Nothing left.Rarely. Last set of an accessory lift at most.
1 to 2 leftHard. Form is holding, speed has dropped.Most working sets. This is the target.
3 to 4 leftSolid work, clearly more in the tank.Heavy skill work and the first sets of a block.

How the weight goes up
  • Beat the target, then add. When you can hit one or two reps past the top of the range on your last set, add 2% to 10% to the bar next time.2 Small jumps on small lifts, bigger jumps on squats and hinges.
  • Add sets before you add days. More weekly sets is the volume lever that actually moves size.4 Add a set or two per muscle every week or two, do not double the workload overnight.
  • Back off on purpose. If your top set drops for two sessions running, take a light week: same lifts, about half the sets, keep 4 or more reps in reserve. You come back stronger rather than stalling for a month.
  • Range of motion is part of the load. Full range through the whole rep beats a heavier partial for both strength and size.1
Periodization: moving the dials over time

Periodization just means the plan changes on purpose instead of by accident. You cannot push every dial to the ceiling every week, so you pick what leads and let the rest sit back, then swap. The three shapes below are the ones with real evidence behind them.

Linear · 12 weeks

Volume starts high and light, then walks down as the weight walks up. Best for a beginner or anyone pointed at one date.

What the evidence actually says

Worth being straight about, because the internet is not.

  • A planned program beats an unplanned one for maximal strength, even when the total work is identical.7
  • Linear and undulating come out about even. Trained lifters get a small edge from undulating, beginners do not.7,8
  • For size, the model barely matters. Total hard sets and steady progression do.9
  • ACSM's 2026 review of 137 reviews found complex periodization did not reliably change results for healthy adults. The program you will actually keep doing wins.1
Putting it together
  • Pick the goal that leads for the next 8 to 12 weeks. The others stay in maintenance, they do not disappear.
  • Set the dials from the table on page one, hit every muscle twice a week, and write down what you lifted. The log is the plan.
  • Progress the weight when you beat the range, add sets slowly, and take the light week before your body takes it for you.
  • Twelve honest weeks of a simple plan beats four perfect weeks of a complicated one, every time.
